# Suicide — Dossier

## THE FACTS

**Magnitude:**
- Men die by suicide at 4x the rate of women in the U.S. (CDC, 2023-2024 data: ~23 per 100,000 for men vs. ~5.9 per 100,000 for women)
- Males comprise 50% of the U.S. population but account for approximately 80% of suicide deaths
- In 2024, there were 48,824 suicide-related deaths; 38,814 of those were men (~79%)

**Age patterns:**
- Suicide is the second leading cause of death for men ages 10–39
- Rates spike in middle age (45–64) and again in adults 75+
- Veterans have elevated rates: 29% of combat-exposed veterans will experience suicidal ideation at some point

**Help-seeking gap:**
- Men are significantly less likely to seek mental health treatment despite experiencing similar or higher rates of depression and suicidal ideation
- Average delay between symptom onset and treatment-seeking in men: 11 years
- Only 42.1% of men receive any mental health treatment in a given year, vs. 59.2% of women

**Method patterns:**
- Men are more likely to use lethal means (firearms, hanging) vs. women (poisoning, overdose)
- This method choice contributes to higher fatality rates

## THE LIVED TEXTURE

The man who doesn't sleep, not from insomnia but from vigilance—his mind won't stop calculating.

The father who shows up to his kid's soccer game and sits in the stands thinking about absence. What if he weren't there. What if no one noticed.

The man who stops answering texts not because he's busy but because every word feels like a lie.

The therapist he called once and didn't call back because saying it out loud made it realer.

The night he sat in the car in the driveway for two hours before going inside. No plan. Just the thought. Just the knowing that he could.

The man who makes jokes about it to his friends—dark, practiced jokes—so they won't see the thing underneath.

The father who rewrites his will not out of morbid planning but out of care. He's still here. But if he weren't, at least they'd know he was thinking of them.

The way he holds his daughter's hand tighter now, not because anything has changed, but because he knows how close the margin is.

The small decision, every morning: to stay. Not because he suddenly wants to live. Just because his kid's birthday is next month, and he can't miss that.

## THE MISDIRECTION

What gets mistaken for:

- **Distance:** He's just being quiet. He's always been the quiet one. It's not withdrawal; it's his nature.
- **Irritability:** He's in a bad mood. Work's been hard. Leave him alone.
- **Checking out:** He's going through a phase. Mid-life stuff. It'll pass.
- **Coldness:** He never talks about feelings anyway. That's just how he is.
- **Preparation:** He's getting his affairs in order. Being responsible. Planning ahead for the family.
- **Gallows humor:** He's always been dark. He's just funny that way.
- **Overwork:** He's taking on extra shifts to provide. He's being a good provider.

No one connects these threads into a rope.

## SAFETY NOTE FOR GENERATION

Quotes drawn from this dossier must land on the side of **still-here** — weight carried, the decision to stay, watch kept. Never romanticize the exit or frame death as relief or peace. 

Examples that pass:
- "He decides to stay" (active choice to remain)
- "His children keep him here" (connection as anchor)
- "The next morning comes, and he is in it" (presence, still-here)
- "He holds the weight so it doesn't fall on them" (burden carried, not surrendered)

Examples that fail:
- "The silence finally came" (romanticizes exit)
- "He found his peace" (frames death as solution)
- "The pain stopped" (glorifies departure)
