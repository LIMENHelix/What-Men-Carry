# Grief and Loss — Dossier

## THE FACTS

**Prevalence and timing:**
- Sadness (76%), depression (43%), fatigue (59%), and changes in appetite (48%) are common grief symptoms
- No prescribed timeline for grief; male grief often appears as absence rather than presence
- Prolonged grief disorder (intense grief lasting more than 12 months) affects a significant minority

**Gender differences in grief expression:**
- Men often express grief through action (working, fixing, organizing) rather than emotional processing
- Male grief may manifest as irritability, anger, risk-taking, or substance abuse rather than visible sadness
- Men are frequently misread as "not grieving" when they are actually grieving in ways that don't match cultural expectations

**Coping patterns in men:**
- 41% of men said they could not have gotten through bereavement without alcohol or drugs
- Men turned to alcohol an average of 13 times per month during acute grief
- 24% of men used recreational drugs daily during grief
- 15% of men never resumed normal substance use patterns post-bereavement
- 30% of men found that substance coping actually heightened their grief

**Suicide risk:**
- Grief significantly elevates suicide risk, particularly in men
- While females are more prone to suicidal ideation, males have higher suicide completion rates
- Loss of a child, spouse, or major life figure creates acute vulnerability

**Long-term impact:**
- Unprocessed male grief contributes to depression, isolation, and identity loss
- Some men never fully process grief, instead managing it chronically

## THE LIVED TEXTURE

The day the phone call comes and his life has a before and an after.

The funeral he attends in a suit, standing still while everything inside is moving.

The house that still smells like the person who's gone.

The moment he reaches for his phone to call them, forgetting they won't answer.

The holiday where the empty chair is louder than any noise.

The man who goes to work because staying home is impossible, because people expect him to keep going.

The bottle of beer he has at night, not because he wants it but because it makes the feeling quieter.

The task of going through their things, the weight of every object.

The friend who says "let me know if you need anything" and means it, and the inability to ask.

The way people stop mentioning the person after a few weeks, the unspoken agreement that he should have moved on.

The night he does something reckless—drives too fast, takes a risk—and can't explain why.

The knowing that his grief looks like anger, so no one brings him casseroles anymore.

## THE MISDIRECTION

What gets mistaken for:

- **Lack of feeling:** He's not crying. He must not care. He's cold.
- **Strength:** He's handling it well. He's strong. He's moved on.
- **Avoidance:** He won't talk about it. He's in denial. He's shut down.
- **Addiction:** He's drinking too much. He's got a problem. He's using substances to escape.
- **Anger:** He's angry. He's bitter. He's resentful.
- **Recovery:** He seems fine now. He's better. The grief must be over.

No one sees that grief in a man looks like absence, and absence looks like choice.
