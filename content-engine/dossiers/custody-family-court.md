# Custody and Family Court — Dossier

## THE FACTS

**Custody patterns:**
- Mothers retain primary custody in ~90% of cases where custody is contested
- Fathers often default to non-custodial arrangements due to cost of litigation and legal biases
- Only 22% of custodial parents are fathers, though this is slowly increasing

**Legal dynamics:**
- Men typically face higher attorney costs to fight for equal custody
- Maternal preference bias remains embedded in many court systems, though it is officially gender-neutral
- Evidence of parental fitness is interpreted differently based on gender (e.g., father working long hours = provider; mother working = abandonment)

**Emotional toll:**
- Men forced to defend their parenting capability in court report lasting trauma
- The process of legal custody battles adds years of stress and uncertainty
- Access to children becomes contingent on legal processes, not parental relationship

**Financial consequences:**
- Custody battles can cost $10,000–$50,000+ per case
- Men often cannot afford extended litigation to challenge initial rulings
- Long-term child support obligations reduce economic mobility

**Post-judgment outcomes:**
- Men with less-favorable custody arrangements report lower quality of life, higher depression, elevated suicide risk
- Limited custody creates barriers to parental involvement in child's education and daily decisions
- Some men withdraw entirely, finding the legal barrier insurmountable

## THE LIVED TEXTURE

The man in the waiting room of the courthouse, meeting his lawyer two days before trial, seeing the stack of papers arguing he is less capable than the person he chose to marry.

The judge who doesn't look at him when she speaks.

The therapist mandated by the court who he has to convince, month after month, that he loves his children and didn't damage them.

The photos his ex introduces as evidence—him coaching the soccer game, him at the school pickup—presented as proof of his inability to parent, reframed as interference.

The day the decision comes down: he gets them six days per month, if scheduled in advance.

The questions his daughter asks in court, the lawyer feeding her words to say about him.

The supervised visitation center, a room with surveillance, the shame of being watched with his own children.

The text message: "She doesn't want to go to your house this weekend."

The attorney bills that keep coming, the judgment against him growing heavier with each invoice.

The knowledge that he cannot make decisions about school, medical care, or religion—he can only watch them be made.

## THE MISDIRECTION

What gets mistaken for:

- **Lack of interest:** He's not fighting for custody, so he must not want it. He's given up.
- **Unreliability:** He doesn't call enough. He's not involved. He's a part-time parent.
- **Financial irresponsibility:** He's bitter about paying child support. He's not contributing.
- **Absence:** He's not around enough to know his kids. He's a stranger to them.
- **Acceptance:** He's fine with how things are. He's moved on. He's adjusted.
- **Manipulation:** He's using his children as leverage. He's trying to turn them against their mother.

No one sees the letters he drafted to the judge that he never sent, the fight he stopped fighting because it hurt the kids to keep fighting it.
