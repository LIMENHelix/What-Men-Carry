# Fatherhood — Dossier

## THE FACTS

**Prevalence of perinatal mental health issues:**
- 8% of new fathers experience clinical depression during the perinatal period (weeks before and after birth)
- 11% experience anxiety; 6–9% experience elevated stress
- About 1 in 10 fathers experience Paternal Postpartum Depression (PPPD)
- Up to 16% deal with an anxiety disorder in the perinatal period

**Sources of parenting stress:**
- Sleep deprivation, adjustment to new routines, financial concerns, work-life balance
- 44% of men report burnout, defined as chronic stress and exhaustion
- Identity shifts from individual/partner to provider/protector/parent

**Stigma and help-seeking:**
- The stigma of emotional difficulty in early parenthood is even higher for men than women
- Cultural messaging ("be strong") creates shame and prevents help-seeking
- Many fathers suffer in silence, fearing they will be judged as weak

**Impact on child development:**
- Poor paternal mental health directly affects parenting quality and child neurodevelopment
- Children with depressed fathers show measurable developmental delays
- The father's emotional availability is as critical as the mother's, but often overlooked

**Work-life conflict:**
- New fathers report higher work stress as they attempt to "provide" while also being present
- Economic pressure creates a false binary: be a good provider or be a good father

## THE LIVED TEXTURE

The night he holds his newborn for the first time and feels the weight of everything he has to protect it from.

The 3 AM feeding when his wife is asleep and it's just him and the small mouth and the realization that he will never again be only responsible for himself.

The conversation with his boss about taking paternity leave, the sense that he is already failing because he needs time away.

The feeling in his chest when his baby cries and he doesn't know why, when he tries everything and nothing works.

The mask he wears at work—competent, unaffected—and the mask he wears at home—engaged, patient—while underneath both is the constant arithmetic of not enough money, not enough sleep, not enough of himself.

The way his wife looks at him when he says he's tired, the silent judgment that he should be stronger than this.

The grandfather who says, "Your dad was at work when you were born; we didn't sit around holding the baby."

The question: If I fail my child, what have I failed?

The moment he realizes his kid prefers his wife, and the knowledge that this is natural and also somehow his fault.

The small boy who says, "Mommy is better at this," and he accepts it because maybe she is, maybe he is a backup parent.

## THE MISDIRECTION

What gets mistaken for:

- **Weakness:** He's struggling with the transition. He needs to toughen up.
- **Disinterest:** He's not engaged with the baby. He's not a natural father.
- **Resentment:** He's angry about his life changing. He's selfish.
- **Detachment:** He's not bonding with the baby. There's something wrong with him.
- **Incompetence:** He can't do basic care tasks. He's incapable.
- **Laziness:** He's tired all the time. He's making excuses.

No one recognizes that fatherhood broke something open in him, and he's trying to hold it together while everyone watches.
